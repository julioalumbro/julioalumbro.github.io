About the Dataset

The course weather dataset contains daily measurements from NOAA weather stations from January 1 through September 21, 2017. This project uses temperature, precipitation, snow, and wind measurements for U.S. states and territories. To make the dataset small enough for a webpage, available station measurements were averaged for each state and date.

Design Decisions

A line chart was chosen because position along a shared time axis makes daily and seasonal changes easy to see. The state, measurement, and month dropdowns provide dynamic filtering. Hovering over a point provides details on demand, while the high and low annotations call attention to unusual days. We considered a map, but a map would make changes over time harder to compare. We also avoided showing every station because that would make the chart crowded and difficult to read.

Development Process

Our project was developed through several stages of planning, data preparation, implementation, and refinement. We began by exploring the available datasets and collectively evaluating which one would be the most interesting and suitable for our visualization. After discussing our options, we decided to focus on the weather dataset. Once we selected our dataset, we established a development plan and divided responsibilities among team members. Matthew and Ben focused on data wrangling, transformation, and cleaning, while Julio Gabriel (Gabe) focused on developing the visualization using D3.js. We began the implementation process by creating a basic line chart with D3.js and gradually incorporating interactive features. Once the data was cleaned and structured, we developed the HTML layout and implemented dropdown menus to allow users to filter the data. Through several iterations, we refined the visualization's layout, functionality, and overall presentation. We also added a chart title, description, and summary to provide users with additional context. To further improve interactivity, Matthew implemented dynamic filtering features, including an additional dropdown for selecting months. He also updated the chart title and description to dynamically reflect the selected state, measurement, and month. During the final stages of development, all team members collaborated on styling, refining the user experience, and completing the project write-up. Gabe was responsible for documenting the development process and deploying the final visualization, while Ben created the demonstration video showcasing the project's features and functionality. This process roughly took 10-15 people hours of work, with each member spending around the same amount of time on the project. We also had several group meetings to discuss our progress and make decisions about the project. In terms of what took most of our time, we spent a significant amount of time on data wrangling and cleaning, as well as implementing the improved interactive features of the visualization. some features of the visualization were a lot easier to implement as we followed instructions from previous labs, but we also had to figure out how to make them unique instead of copying existing implementations, which took more time. Overall, our development process emphasized collaboration, iterative improvements, and the integration of data processing and interactive visualization techniques to create an informative and user-friendly weather visualization.
